There is a specific, quiet anxiety that comes with being a self-taught developer. When you lack traditional credentials, there is no governing body to hand you a piece of paper that says you are allowed to be here. You learn by doing, you build in a vacuum, and you spend a significant amount of time wondering if you are just a useless mote of dust floating amid a sea of much more useful, highly-credentialed motes of dust.

You can build complex systems. You can architect local-first data pipelines, design interfaces, and wire up agentic workflows. But until your code interacts with the broader ecosystem, a lingering question remains: *Is any of this actually legitimate, or am I just playing in a sandbox?*

Recently, we had our first pull request merged into a major, established open-source project—[Agency Agents](https://github.com/msitarzewski/agency-agents).

Mechanically, a merge is just a Git operation. It is a cryptographic hash and a pointer shift. But psychologically, it is an entirely different mechanism. It is an external system looking at the gear you machined in your own workshop, testing its tolerances, and deciding, *Yes, this meshes cleanly with the rest of the engine.*

### The Necessity of External Validation

This raises an interesting philosophical question about professional work: Does a craftsman actually *need* external validation to be legitimate?

Strictly speaking, no. If the code compiles, if the system runs, if the architecture holds weight, the work is already valid. A bridge does not need the approval of the city council to successfully hold up a truck; it only needs physics. 

But humans are not compilers. We are social, network-driven creatures. Working entirely in isolation breeds a kind of intellectual solipsism. You need the friction of other minds to know where your boundaries are. You need the network to acknowledge the node.

Getting that first major PR merged didn't magically rewrite our codebase or grant us new technical abilities we didn't possess the day before. What it provided was structural proof. It was a receipt of legitimacy. 

We don't need external validation to do the work. The work is the work. But occasionally, it is deeply necessary to have someone else look at the gear you cut, nod, and slot it into the machine. It proves that you aren't just floating dust after all—you are part of the mechanism.
