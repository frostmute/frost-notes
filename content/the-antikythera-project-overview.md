If you arrived here expecting a standard feed of chronological blog posts, the interface likely caught you off guard. You are looking at a dial, a set of concentric rings, and a collection of glowing nodes that spin when you drag them.

This site is not a conventional blog, and it does not use a conventional database. It is a portfolio, a reliquary of essays, and a laboratory. But more importantly, the site itself is an instrument—and its shape is entirely deliberate.

To understand why it works the way it does, you first need to understand its namesake.

### The Original Mechanism

In 1901, sponge divers off the coast of the Greek island of Antikythera discovered a corroded lump of bronze in an ancient shipwreck. When scholars finally x-rayed it decades later, they found something impossible: a highly complex, hand-cranked analog computer containing dozens of precision gear wheels. 

Built over two thousand years ago, the Antikythera mechanism was used to calculate and predict astronomical positions, eclipses, and orbits. It was a physical model of the cosmos, driven entirely by the mathematical relationships between its gears.

### The Digital Translation

This website is a digital homage to that idea. Rather than using an opaque algorithm to serve you content, or a hidden backend database to organize it, the site uses visible, mathematical mechanics.

The "gimmick" of the site is that **everything you see on the dial is computed directly from the text files themselves.**

When you load the page, there is no server constructing the layout. Instead, a lightweight script reads a collection of plain Markdown files (the essays, projects, and notes). It looks at the small block of metadata at the top of each file—things like the publication date, the category, and the word count—and uses that data as the "gears" to physically place the article on the screen.

- **The Angle:** The exact day of the year the article was published dictates its rotation around the dial.
- **The Rings:** The category (whether it is an instrument, a quick field note, or a long philosophical essay) places it on a specific colored orbit.
- **The Sigils:** The unique geometric symbol next to an article is generated mathematically from the letters in its title.
- **The Resonance:** The dashed lines connecting certain nodes represent cross-references between related articles, forming constellations of thought.

The interface is not just a stylistic overlay; it is a literal readout of the data underneath.

### The Meta: Why Build It This Way?

Most of the modern web is designed to hide its machinery from you. You scroll through an endless feed, and an invisible server decides what you see next based on engagement metrics. Your data, your writing, and your history are locked inside someone else's database.

This project is a rejection of that model. It is built on a philosophy of **data sovereignty and local-first craftsmanship.** 

Because there is no database and no complex server framework, the site cannot be broken by a software update. The articles are just plain text files sitting in a folder. If the internet were to shut down tomorrow, the entire archive could still be read on a local computer without needing to "log in" or spin up a cloud environment. The Javascript that draws the dial is just the "brass casing" built around those text files to make them beautiful and explorable.

The form of the site *is* its function. By shaping the archive like an instrument, it forces you to consciously navigate it. You have to turn the dial. You have to follow the resonance lines that connect related thoughts. 

It invites you to look at the work not as a fleeting feed of content to be consumed, but as a durable, mechanical system of ideas meant to be explored.

### The Mechanism and the Maker

Ultimately, this site is not just a container for a portfolio; the site *is* the portfolio. 

As a dual-specialized creative designer and technology expert—someone who splits his time between the physical craftsmanship of design and the abstract architecture of AI software development—I needed a space that reflected my personal philosophy. I am interested in how things are built, how systems interact, and how we can preserve the artifact of human intention in an increasingly automated web.

My name is Jonathan J Wagner, and this archive is the truest representation of how my mind works. The articles and projects you find here—whether they are photography collections, field notes on agentic systems, or physical print mockups—are the individual gears. The site itself is the machine I built to hold them together. 

By demanding that the interface be driven purely by the files themselves, and by refusing to hide the machinery behind a slick, templated framework, I am making an argument through software. The form and function of the Antikythera mechanism are indistinguishable from the work it presents. It is a living, functional piece of design that requires you to reach out, take hold of the mechanism, and turn the dial.
